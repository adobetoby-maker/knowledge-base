# SEO Content Writing Guide — Voice, Quality, and Output Standards

## Voice Standards by Project

### Jr.'s Auto Repair
- **Tone:** Knowledgeable neighbor, not corporate
- **Voice:** Pablo is a mechanic who's been in Twin Falls for 13+ years. He speaks directly and honestly.
- **Avoid:** Insurance-style hedging ("may," "might," "could potentially"), corporate speak, excessive disclaimers
- **Use:** Specific numbers, direct advice, local references, plain language

Example contrast:
- Weak: "Vehicle owners may want to consider having their brake systems inspected periodically to ensure optimal performance."
- Strong: "Check your brakes every 12,000 miles, or sooner if you hear grinding. Ignoring it turns a $200 pad job into a $600 rotor replacement."

### Climb Sites (Brasil, Spain, Utah, Kalymnos)
- **Tone:** Excited local guide
- **Voice:** Someone who's climbed there and knows the beta
- **Use:** Climbing-specific terminology, route names, grades (Yosemite Decimal for Utah, French for Brazil/Spain/Kalymnos)
- **Avoid:** Generic travel writing; clichés like "breathtaking views" without specifics

### LinguaLens / Language Sites
- **Tone:** Supportive tutor
- **Voice:** Patient, encouraging, practical — helps learners feel capable
- **Use:** Learning science references, example sentences, relatable learner struggles

## Quality Standards

### Specificity Rule
Every claim must be backed by a specific fact, number, or example:
- "Brake pads last 30,000–70,000 miles depending on driving style" → specific range
- "Twin Falls has 300 sunny days per year" → specific fact
- "Kalymnos has 3,000+ routes on 11 different crags" → specific count

Generic claims ("great service," "beautiful destination," "effective learning") are SEO dead weight and reader turn-offs.

### The 80% Reader Test
After writing, ask: would a reader know more about the specific topic after reading this than before? If the answer is "they could have found this anywhere," the content has no differentiation signal. Search engines increasingly reward specificity.

### First-Draft vs Published Standard

First draft: capture the information in the correct structure.

Published standard:
- Each section has at least one specific fact, number, or example
- At least one original observation (not just restatement of common knowledge)
- Local connection (city, region, or project-specific context) where relevant
- No filler phrases: "In today's world," "It goes without saying," "At the end of the day"

## Content Gap Analysis

Before writing, check `lib/articles.ts` for the project to see existing content. The gap analysis:

1. What service pages exist without supporting blog content?
2. What questions do customers frequently ask that have no article?
3. What do competitors rank for that you don't?
4. What seasonal topics are coming up in the next 30 days?

## The Snippet Formula

For featured snippet targeting, every article should have one section formatted as:

For "what is" / definition questions:
```
**[Term]** is [definition in 1-2 sentences]. [Expand with 1 specific detail]. [Local context if applicable].
```

For "how to" questions (numbered steps):
```
**How to [Action]:**
1. [Step] — [brief explanation]
2. [Step] — [brief explanation]
3. [Step] — [brief explanation]
```

For "how much" / cost questions:
```
**[Service] in [City] costs [range] on average.** [Factors that affect price]. [What's included]. [Call to action].
```

## Word Count Targets

| Content Type | Target Length |
|--------------|---------------|
| Service page | 500–800 words |
| Blog article (informational) | 800–1,500 words |
| Blog article (how-to) | 1,000–2,000 words |
| FAQ page | 600–1,200 words |
| Homepage | 400–600 words |

Longer is not automatically better. Fill length with useful specifics; never pad.

## Fact-Checking Requirements

For automotive content: verify service intervals against manufacturer specs (not just "industry standard").

For climbing content: verify route grades and approach times against current guidebook editions or Mountain Project.

For language content: verify grammar rules against authoritative source (university textbook, official language academy) — learners spread incorrect corrections rapidly.

When in doubt, soften the claim rather than state incorrect facts: "typically" / "often" / "most commonly" buys wiggle room without lying.
